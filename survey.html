Where do you sit?

Three sets of questions, taken in order or stopped at any point. One: fourteen statements matching the spectrum, one per axis. Two: thirty-six more, drawing the finer distinctions that separate denominations. Three: eleven questions that cannot be charted at all, reported without a score alongside what various traditions teach. Each set begins with the questions most Christians share and moves toward those that divide traditions most sharply; within each stretch the order is shuffled. The first set takes about three minutes. Nothing is stored, sent, or seen by anyone but you.

Questions that cannot be charted

Eleven questions that do not sit on any axis — because traditions divide over whether the question even applies, because almost none has an official position, or because one tradition alone teaches it. Nothing here produces an alignment score. Your answers come back with what various traditions actually teach, and with a note on why each question resists the chart.

Alignment by spectrum

The axes fall into three conceptual groups, with Sabbath on its own and the new baptism-candidates axis apart. The grouping is a reading aid, not a finding; the chart page shows the two clusters the data actually produce. Alignment is computed separately for each, because a person can sit close to one tradition on authority and close to another entirely on practice. The nearest three in each are shown.

What is wrong with this survey

Stated plainly, because you should know it before you weigh the result.

  • The three spectrums are partly imposed. Correlating the axes across the traditions produces a split into two groups, not three. The three-way arrangement is a presentational choice laid over that, not a finding. Sabbath fits neither and is now reported on its own.
  • Question order can push answers. The order runs from widely shared beliefs to divisive ones, so that no single tradition’s distinctive claim comes first, and it is shuffled within each stretch. That reduces order effects; it does not remove them.
  • A five-point scale flattens real distinctions. Somewhat agree covers a great deal of ground, and two people choosing it may hold very different views.
  • The traditions are charted by judgment, not by measurement. Alignment is only as good as those placements, and some are wrong.
  • Broad labels hide internal disagreement. Version 7 splits the Lutherans (ELCA, LCMS, WELS) and the Quakers (programmed, unprogrammed), but other labels, Presbyterian / Reformed among them, still cover bodies that disagree.
  • The author is not neutral. This was built by a Latter-day Saint attorney, and the statements were written by one hand.
  • Nothing here is a verdict. A close match means your stated answers and a tradition's charted scores sit near one another in this draft. It is not a claim about where you belong.

On the method. Each answer maps to a score from 0 to 100 on its axis: strongly agree and strongly disagree at the ends, neutral at the midpoint. Some statements are worded in reverse, so that agreeing with everything does not push you steadily toward one end. An axis score is the mean of the statements you answered for that axis. Total alignment is 100 minus the mean absolute difference between your axis scores and a tradition’s charted scores, across answered axes only. Skipped axes are excluded rather than treated as neutral.

On the limits. The charted scores are judgments, better read as ordinal than cardinal. Several labels still cover internally diverse families, so alignment with a broad label may mean less than it appears. Where an axis does not apply to a tradition — baptism candidates where there is no water baptism — that axis is left out of that tradition’s alignment rather than scored. Corrections to any placement are welcome.
