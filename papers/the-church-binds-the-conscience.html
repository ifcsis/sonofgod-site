The Church Binds the Conscience, the State Binds the Estate

Marriage as Incorporation

By S. Harrison Knudson, J.D.

The wrong fight

The state has two powers over domestic life. One is the power to enforce obligations: support, property, the care of children. The other is the power to define what a relationship means — whether it is a marriage at all. The first is ordinary government. The second is a question of conscience, and the state answers it every time it licenses a union or asks on a form whether a person is married.

The American argument over marriage has been an argument over who may enter the category. This article asks the prior question: why the state maintains the category at all.

The proposal

First, a narrow step. A religious ceremony should have no civil effect on its own. Alabama has already moved this way, replacing marriage licenses with a recorded form in 2019, and much of continental Europe keeps civil registration separate from the rite.

Second, the contribution. Replace marital status with registered domestic incorporation: people register the household they are actually forming, choose among a small menu of standard forms, and sit on a floor no agreement can waive — support for children, recognition of caregiving, full disclosure, and protection against coercion and abuse.

The hard question

Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) holds that marriage is a fundamental right. Whether a state could honor that right while withdrawing civil marriage from everyone is unanswered by precedent, and the article says so. If the answer is no, the framework survives in narrower form, with civil marriage kept as one standard form among several.

In a sentence

The church binds the conscience. The state binds the estate.
